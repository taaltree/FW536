Problem 1 · hierarchical Poisson · ants.csv

Is there extra variation in ant richness? An observation-level random effect

The data. ants.csv records ant species richness (the number of ant species found) at 22 sites across New England. At each site ants were sampled twice, once in a bog and once in the forest next to it, so the file has 44 rows. The covariates are forest (1 for the forest sample, 0 for the bog sample), latitude, and elevation. This is the dataset from section A of the lab.

Why this problem. A Poisson regression assumes the variance of the counts equals their mean. Raw richness does not look like that: its mean is 7.0 and its variance is 18.0. Some of that extra spread is expected, though, because habitat, latitude, and elevation shift the mean from row to row. The real question is whether any extra variation is left over once the covariates are in the model. If there is, a plain Poisson regression is overconfident: its standard errors and credible intervals come out too narrow.

One way to find out, and to fix it if needed, is to give every row its own small random nudge on the log mean, $\epsilon_i$. You met this device in Day 3, Problem 6, as an observation-level random effect: "observation-level" because there is one $\epsilon_i$ per row of data, not one per site or group. The nudges are drawn from a Normal distribution with standard deviation $\sigma_\epsilon$, and $\sigma_\epsilon$ is the answer to the question. A $\sigma_\epsilon$ near 0 means the covariates already account for the spread and plain Poisson was fine; a clearly positive $\sigma_\epsilon$ measures how much extra variation there is.

$y_i \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_i), \quad \log \lambda_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{forest}_i + \beta_2 \text{lat}_i + \beta_3 \text{elev}_i + \epsilon_i, \quad \epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_\epsilon^2)$
R · start from the real data
ants <- read.csv(here::here("Day5_BayesII", "data", "ants.csv"))
ants$forest    <- as.numeric(scale(ants$forest))
ants$latitude  <- as.numeric(scale(ants$latitude))
ants$elevation <- as.numeric(scale(ants$elevation))
  1. Fit the model in NIMBLE with weakly informative priors ($\beta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2{=}4)$, $\sigma_\epsilon \sim \text{Uniform}(0,5)$). Report posterior medians and 95% CIs for $\beta_1,\beta_2,\beta_3$ and for $\sigma_\epsilon$.
  2. Look at the posterior of $\sigma_\epsilon$. Does it point to meaningful leftover variation, or is it consistent with values close to 0? What does that tell you about overdispersion once the covariates are in the model?
  3. Now fit the plain Poisson regression without the random effect, glm(richness ~ forest + latitude + elevation, family = poisson), using the scaled covariates.
    1. Compare your $\beta$ posterior medians and intervals to the glm() estimates. Where do they agree or differ, and why?
    2. Is there evidence of overdispersion from the glm() fit? Divide its residual deviance by its residual degrees of freedom; a ratio near 1 means no overdispersion, and a ratio well above 1 means the counts vary more than Poisson allows (Day 3, Problem 6).
    3. Does that agree with what you inferred from $\sigma_\epsilon$ in the Bayesian model in (b)? Was the random effect needed?

Problem 2 · what a prior actually claims

What does a "vague" log-scale prior actually assert?

This problem picks the M1 draw back up and takes it two steps further, onto a per-unit effect you can state in a sentence, and into an argument you would actually have with a reviewer. On a log link a prior on $\beta$ is a prior on a multiplicative effect $e^\beta$, and that translation is where "uninformative" priors stop being uninformative.

Problem 3 · multilevel pooling · N2OEmission.csv

The pooling spectrum for soil N2O emissions

The data. Nitrous oxide (N2O) is a potent greenhouse gas, and fertilized farm soils are a major source. N2OEmission.csv compiles 563 emission measurements from 107 sites. Each row is one measurement: a fertilizer applied at some rate at one site, and the N2O emission that followed. The question running through Problems 3 and 4 is how emissions respond to the amount of nitrogen applied, and how much that differs among sites and fertilizers.

  • emission: the N2O emission measured. The response, used as $y_i = \log(\text{emission})$.
  • n.input: the amount of nitrogen fertilizer applied. The predictor, used as $x_i = \log(\text{n.input})$ minus its mean. Because both are logged, the slope $\beta$ is unit-free: $\beta = 1$ means doubling the fertilizer doubles the emission.
  • group.index: which of the 107 sites the row comes from, numbered 1–107 (group holds the original site IDs). This is the site index $j$, so row $i$ belongs to site $j[i]$. Sites have between 1 and 25 measurements.
  • fert.index: which of 10 fertilizer types was applied, numbered 1–10 (fertilizer holds the original letter codes). This is the fertilizer index $k$. It is not used until Problem 4(b).
  • carbon: soil organic carbon at the site, as a percentage. It is not used until Problem 4.
  • reps: a replicate count carried over from the source compilation. Not used.

Model $\log(\text{emission})$ as a function of centred $\log(\text{n.input})$, with sites indexed by group.index. Section C of the practice lab has the same data and a fuller description.

group <- N2O$group.index
nsites <- length(unique(group))

  3. Report the posterior of $\sigma_\alpha$. In plain language, what does its magnitude say about how different the sites are once N input is accounted for?

Problem 4 · group-level covariate · random slopes · N2OEmission.csv

A site-level covariate on the random intercept, and a new random slope by fertilizer type

Every Bayesian hierarchical model so far today, the lab's N2O models and Problem 3, has had random intercepts only. In Problem 3 each site got its own intercept $\alpha_j$, but all 563 measurements shared one N-input slope $\beta$: every site and every fertilizer was assumed to respond to added nitrogen at the same rate. Problem 3's model also says that sites differ, through $\sigma_\alpha$, but not why. This problem changes both, in one model that you fit once. It adds two pieces of structure at the same time, and they use two different columns, so keep them separate:

  • A site-level covariate on the random intercept, to explain site differences with soil carbon. The site-level covariate is $w_j$, one value per site, built from the carbon column. Average carbon within each site to get $c_j$, the site's percent soil organic carbon (36 sites report more than one value), then take its logit, $w_j = \log\big(c_j/(100-c_j)\big)$. State how you scaled $w_j$ (for example, standardized to mean 0 and SD 1).
  • A random slope, so the N-input slope differs by fertilizer type. This is new for today. Until now only intercepts varied by group; here the slope becomes a random effect too. You fit random slopes with lmer() on Day 3; this is the same idea written out in NIMBLE. The single $\beta$ is replaced by ten fertilizer-specific slopes $\beta_k$, each drawn from a shared distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu_\beta, \sigma_\beta^2)$, so they are partially pooled toward the average slope $\mu_\beta$ exactly the way site intercepts are pooled toward their mean. The grouping for the slope is fert.index, not the site and not $w_j$. Fertilizer type is recorded per measurement, not per site (47 sites tested more than one type), so each row $i$ gets the slope of its own type, $\beta_{k[i]}$ with $k[i]$ = fert.index.

The full model, with both additions, written out with $j[i]$ = group.index and $k[i]$ = fert.index for row $i$:

$y_i \sim \mathcal{N}\big(\alpha_{j[i]} + \beta_{k[i]}\, x_i,\ \sigma^2\big), \qquad \alpha_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\kappa + \eta\, w_j,\ \sigma_\alpha^2), \qquad \beta_{k} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_\beta, \sigma_\beta^2)$
  • $\kappa$ (kappa) and $\eta$ (eta) are the intercept and slope of a regression of the site intercepts on soil carbon. $\kappa$ is the expected $\alpha_j$ for a site with $w_j = 0$ (average carbon, once $w$ is standardized), and it replaces Problem 3's $\mu_\alpha$. $\eta$ is how much a site's typical log-emission changes per unit of $w_j$.
  • $\sigma_\alpha$ is the scatter of site intercepts around that line, the site variation carbon does not explain.
  • $\mu_\beta$ is the average N-input slope across fertilizer types, and $\sigma_\beta$ is how much the ten type-specific slopes $\beta_k$ differ from it.

Fit this model once. Part (a) interprets the site-level effect of soil carbon from that fit, and part (b) interprets the random slope from the same fit.

A group-level covariate. Here the site intercept is no longer a black-box draw from $\mathcal{N}(\mu_\alpha,\sigma_\alpha^2)$ but is given a group-level predictor $w_j$. Writing it as a regression on the intercept, $\alpha_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\kappa+\eta\, w_j, \sigma_\alpha^2)$, is the same model as carrying $w_j$ as an ordinary term in the linear predictor with a mean-zero site random effect: two notations, one likelihood and one set of predictions. What the covariate buys you (versus a random intercept with no covariate) is real either way. It explains why sites differ (through the posterior of $\eta$), it sharpens shrinkage (a data-poor site is pulled toward what its own covariate predicts, not just the grand mean), and it lets you predict the baseline emission of an unsampled site from its covariate. See section C of the practice lab for the worked intuition.

And why let the slope vary? A single $\beta$ asserts that every fertilizer converts nitrogen input into emissions at the same rate. That is a strong claim, and it is one you make by accident every time you fit one slope to pooled data. Letting $\beta_k$ vary buys three things. It lets you ask a question the pooled model cannot phrase (does the response depend on what you spread?), it stops a heavily-sampled fertilizer from setting the slope for all the others, and it makes $\sigma_\beta$ itself an answer, since a small $\sigma_\beta$ is positive evidence that the pooled model was fine. Notice the price. Ten slopes are estimated from between 12 and 161 observations each. That is precisely the setting in which the largest estimate is most often just the noisiest group, which is what part (b) makes you check.

  1. Fit the full model above in NIMBLE, with both the soil-carbon covariate on the site intercepts and the random slope by fertilizer type. Site-level effect of soil carbon: report the posterior median, 95% credible interval, and $\Pr(\eta > 0)$ for $\eta$. Is there evidence that soil carbon affects baseline emissions, and in which direction? Say how big the effect is in plain terms.
  2. Random slope by fertilizer type, from the same fit: report the posterior of $\sigma_\beta$ and rank the ten types by posterior median slope. Then test the ranking before you believe it: across your draws, count how often the top-ranked type actually holds the largest $\beta_k$ of the ten, and report that probability together with the type's 95% CI and the number of measurements it rests on. On this evidence, would you write “fertilizer type X produces the steepest emissions response” in a paper? One or two sentences.

Problem 6 · spawner-recruit + derived $S_\text{msy}$

Beverton-Holt fit with management quantities

Simulate a spawner-recruit time series from a known truth, then fit the Beverton-Holt model in NIMBLE and define the management quantities as deterministic nodes so their posteriors come for free.

$\log R_t \sim \mathcal{N}\!\big(\log a + \log S_t - \log(1 + \tfrac{a}{b} S_t),\ \sigma^2\big)$
$N_\text{eq} = \dfrac{b(a-1)}{a}, \quad S_\text{msy} = b\sqrt{\tfrac1a} - \tfrac{b}{a}, \quad H_\text{msy} = 1 - \sqrt{\tfrac1a}$
R · simulate 25 years of data
set.seed(7)
Nyears <- 25; a.t <- 5; b.t <- 5000; sig.t <- 0.3
S <- rlnorm(Nyears, 7.5, 0.8)
R <- numeric(Nyears)
for (t in 1:Nyears)
  R[t] <- (a.t*S[t])/(1 + a.t/b.t*S[t]) * exp(rnorm(1, 0, sig.t))

What this problem is for. Nobody manages a fishery by $a$ and $b$. They manage by an escapement target and a harvest rate, which are nonlinear functions of $a$ and $b$, and once those functions sit inside the model as deterministic nodes, NIMBLE evaluates them at every draw and hands you their full posteriors for free, correlations and all. The walkthrough fitted the curve; this problem is about what you can then say to a manager with those posteriors.

  1. Fit, with the reference points inside the model. Monitor $a,b,\sigma,N_\text{eq},S_\text{msy},H_\text{msy}$. Before you look at the output, work out the three true reference points by hand, substituting $a=5$ and $b=5000$ into the formulas above. Doing that arithmetic yourself is how you sanity-check any assessment. Report posterior medians and 95% CIs for all six quantities next to your hand-computed truths. Does every interval cover?
  2. A harvest rate with a risk statement attached. The posterior median of $H_\text{msy}$ is, by construction, the harvest rate with a 50% chance of exceeding the true one. Report the 5th, 10th, 25th and 50th percentiles of the posterior. Which would you advise, and write the one-sentence risk statement that has to travel with it.
  3. An escapement target. Report $S_\text{msy}$'s posterior median and 95% CI, and compute $\Pr(S_\text{msy} > 1000)$, $\Pr(S_\text{msy} > 1500)$ and $\Pr(S_\text{msy} > 2000)$. A manager asks you for “the” escapement target. Answer them in two sentences that use these numbers rather than hiding them.

Problem 7 · derived quantities, done properly

Why you cannot build a derived quantity from summary numbers

You have posterior draws of $a$ and $b$ from Problem 6. Management quantities are functions of those parameters, and there are two ways to produce an interval for them, one right and one that looks reasonable and is not.

  1. Compute $S_\text{msy} = b\sqrt{1/a} - b/a$ once per draw, then take the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles of the result. Now do it the other way. Take the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles of $a$ and of $b$ separately, and substitute those numbers into the formula. Report both sets of three numbers and the percentage difference at the 5th percentile.
  2. Explain why the two disagree. What does cor(a, b) across your draws have to do with it, and why does summarising each parameter first throw away exactly the information the derived quantity needs?
  3. Now repeat the comparison for $H_\text{msy} = 1 - \sqrt{1/a}$. The two approaches agree here, to the digit. What is different about this quantity, and what does that tell you about when the shortcut is safe? State the general rule in one sentence.
Watch out. The disagreement in (a) is not large, single-digit percentages on these data. The size is not the lesson; the reason is. A quantity built from several correlated parameters can be badly wrong this way when the correlation is strong or the function is sharply curved, and you will not get a warning.

Problem 8 · N-mixture + Bayesian p-value

Repeat-count abundance and a goodness-of-fit check

An N-mixture model estimates the abundance of unmarked animals when you cannot count every individual. Repeated visits to the same closed site let it separate true abundance ($\lambda$) from detection probability ($p$), which a raw count confounds. (It is the abundance analog of the occupancy model; see the practice lab for a fuller explanation.) Here you will simulate repeated counts, fit the Royle (2004) N-mixture model in NIMBLE, and then test the Poisson-abundance assumption with a posterior-predictive Bayesian p-value.

$N_i \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda), \quad y_{ij} \sim \text{Binomial}(N_i, p)$
R · simulate overdispersed abundance on purpose
library(MASS)
set.seed(9)
sites <- 150; reps <- 3; lambda <- 6; p.true <- 0.5
N <- rnegbin(sites, mu = lambda, theta = 2)   # NB, not Poisson
y <- matrix(NA, sites, reps)
for (i in 1:sites) y[i, ] <- rbinom(reps, N[i], p.true)
  1. Fit the basic (constant $\lambda$, constant $p$) N-mixture. Remember that NIMBLE's binomial is dbinom(prob, size), the reverse of R's argument order. Give the latent abundances starting values with N = apply(y, 1, max). Here y is the sites × visits matrix of counts, the 1 means "work across each row" (each site), and max is R's ordinary max() function, so this returns the largest count seen at each site on any visit. The purpose: a binomial count can never exceed its size, so each site's true abundance $N_i$ must start at least as large as every count recorded there. Start it lower and that count is impossible, its log-probability is $-\infty$, and the sampler cannot begin from that point. (See the apply family tutorial for apply().) Report the posteriors of $\lambda$ and $p$.
  2. Add posterior-predictive replicates $y^\text{rep}$ and a discrepancy statistic (e.g. a Freeman-Tukey or variance/mean statistic). Compute the Bayesian p-value $p_B$. What does a value far from 0.5 indicate here?
  3. Propose (in words, or as a modified nimbleCode block) a fix, e.g. a Poisson-lognormal random effect on $\log\lambda_i$, and explain how it would move $p_B$ toward 0.5.

Problem 9 · latent state · dynamic occupancy

Multi-season occupancy: recover persistence and colonization

Simulate a dynamic (multi-season) occupancy dataset and recover the year-specific persistence $\phi_t$ and colonization $\gamma_t$ from replicated detection/non-detection data with imperfect detection.

$z_{i,t} \sim \text{Bernoulli}\big(z_{i,t-1}\phi_{t-1} + (1-z_{i,t-1})\gamma_{t-1}\big), \quad y_{i,j,t} \sim \text{Bernoulli}(z_{i,t}\, p)$
R · simulate a rising-colonization scenario
set.seed(10)
nS <- 50; nY <- 6; nR <- 3; p.t <- 0.5
phi.t <- c(0.70, 0.70, 0.80, 0.85, 0.85)
gam.t <- c(0.05, 0.15, 0.25, 0.30, 0.30)  # rising colonization
z <- matrix(NA, nS, nY); z[, 1] <- rbinom(nS, 1, 0.2)
for (i in 1:nS) for (t in 2:nY)
  z[i,t] <- rbinom(1, 1, z[i,t-1]*phi.t[t-1] + (1-z[i,t-1])*gam.t[t-1])
y <- array(NA, c(nS, nR, nY))
for (t in 1:nY) for (i in 1:nS) for (j in 1:nR)
  y[i,j,t] <- rbinom(1, 1, p.t) * z[i,t]
