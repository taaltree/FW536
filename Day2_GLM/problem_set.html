Problem 1 · continuous distribution choice

Which distribution for a right-skewed body-size sample?

You record body weight (g) for every rainbow trout kept in a 30-day creel survey on a mountain lake. A histogram of the weights has almost no fish near zero. It rises to a single peak at roughly 80 g and then trails off in a long right tail of a few much larger fish approaching a kilogram. The distribution is strictly positive and strongly right-skewed, but its most common value sits well above zero. (The same reasoning applies to tree DBH, seed mass, or any size variable built up by multiplicative growth.)

  1. Which of {Normal, Lognormal, Gamma, Exponential, Beta} would you choose, and what is the data-generating story that justifies it in one sentence? Which candidate does the peak above zero rule out, and why?
  2. If $\log(\text{weight}) \sim \mathcal{N}(5.0,\,0.8^2)$, what is the expected weight on the raw scale? (Recall the lognormal mean is $e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$.)
  3. What is the median weight? Why does it differ from the mean, and which is larger?
  4. If you mistakenly fit a Normal to these weights and reported a 95% prediction interval centered at the mean, why would the lower endpoint be biological nonsense?
Your answer goes in the R Markdown hand-in. Report the distribution you chose with its one-sentence justification, the mean and median, and the "say it as biology" sentence contrasting them.
Problem 2 · Normal vs lognormal regression, allometry

Length–weight allometry and the power law

For 200 Chinook salmon you have fork length (cm) and weight (g). Lengths range 30–110 cm; weights range 250 g to 18 kg. Two analysts fit:

  • Analyst A: lm(weight ~ length)
  • Analyst B: lm(log(weight) ~ log(length))
  1. Describe what Analyst A's residuals-vs-fitted plot would look like, and name the assumption it violates.
  2. Analyst B finds $\hat\beta_1 = 3.05$. Connect this slope to the standard allometric equation $W = a L^b$ and interpret $b$ biologically. Why is $b = 3$ (the "cube law") the natural null?
  3. What multiplicative effect on weight corresponds to a 25% increase in length? To a doubling of length?
  4. Analyst B back-transforms $\hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 \log(L)$ by exponentiating to predict weight at a given length. Read the note below first. Does that back-transformed value estimate the mean or the median weight at that length? Analyst B's residual standard deviation on the log scale is $\hat\sigma = 0.12$. By what percentage does the mean weight exceed the plain back-transformed value? Compare that with the trout in Problem 1, where $\sigma = 0.8$, and say what makes the correction matter so much more there.

Why a back-transformed log model needs a correction. A regression on $\log(W)$ predicts the average of the log weights at a given length. Exponentiating that average does not give the average weight, because exponentiating stretches large values far more than it lifts small ones. Two fish whose log weights sit equally far either side of 5, at 4.2 and 5.8, show why. Exponentiating the middle value gives $e^{5} = 148$ g. The fish themselves weigh $e^{4.2} = 67$ g and $e^{5.8} = 330$ g, and their average is 198 g. The heavy fish pulls the average up by more than the light fish pulls it down, so exponentiating the average log gives something smaller than the average weight. What it does give is the median weight, because exponentiating never changes which value is in the middle.

When the scatter around the line on the log scale is Normal with standard deviation $\sigma$, the average weight is

$$\text{mean} = \exp\!\left(\hat\mu + \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\right) = e^{\hat\mu} \times e^{\sigma^2/2}, \qquad \hat\mu = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 \log(L).$$

The factor $e^{\sigma^2/2}$ is the half-variance correction. It is always above 1, and it grows with the scatter on the log scale: the more individual fish spread around the line, the further the heavy ones drag the average above the median. It is the same $e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$ you used in Problem 1(b).

Your answer goes in the R Markdown hand-in. Give the allometric interpretation of $b = 3.05$, both multiplicative effects, which of mean and median the back-transform estimates, and the size of the correction for $\hat\sigma = 0.12$ and for $\sigma = 0.8$.
Problem 3 · Exponential and Poisson, memorylessness

Bat passes at an acoustic detector

An ultrasonic detector on a forest edge logs every bat that flies past. Through a summer night, passes arrive at random at an average rate of $\lambda = 4$ per hour, each independent of the others. That one assumption describes the same process in two ways.

  • The number of passes in a window of $t$ hours is Poisson with mean $\lambda t$.
  • The waiting time $T$ until the next pass is Exponential with rate $\lambda$, with density $f(t) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t}$ and cumulative distribution function $F(t) = P(T \le t) = 1 - e^{-\lambda t}$ for $t \ge 0$.

Work in hours, converting minutes as you go, so 30 minutes is $t = 0.5$.

  1. What are the mean and the median waiting time between passes, in minutes? (The median is the $t$ at which $F(t) = 0.5$.) Which is larger, and what about the shape of the Exponential makes it so?
  2. Find the probability that 30 minutes go by with no bat at all, in two ways. First use the Poisson, as the probability of zero passes in half an hour. Then use the Exponential, as the probability that the wait for the next pass is longer than half an hour. Explain in one sentence why the two answers have to be identical.
  3. The detector has already been silent for 20 minutes. What is the probability that it stays silent for at least another 30 minutes, that is, $P(T > 50 \text{ min} \mid T > 20 \text{ min})$? Compare your answer with (b). This is memorylessness: under a constant rate, having already waited 20 minutes tells you nothing about how much longer you will wait. In one or two sentences, say what memorylessness requires of the bats, and give one biological reason real bat passes might not be memoryless.
  4. Check the connection by simulation. Generate waiting times, turn them into the clock time of each pass, and count the passes in each hour: 
    set.seed(11)
gaps   <- rexp(5000, rate = 4)                       # waiting times between passes, in hours
times  <- cumsum(gaps)                               # clock time of each pass
times  <- times[times < 1000]                        # keep the first 1000 hours
counts <- tabulate(floor(times) + 1, nbins = 1000)   # passes in each hour
mean(counts); var(counts)
    Report the mean and variance of the hourly counts. Which distribution do those two numbers point to, and why?
The same link sits behind camera-trap density estimation and the herring spawning example from lecture. Counts of events in a window and the waiting times between events are two views of one process, so a claim about one is a claim about the other. When the constant-rate assumption fails in real data, the counts stop looking Poisson, which is what Problem 4 diagnoses.
Your answer goes in the R Markdown hand-in. Report the mean and median waits, both versions of the 30-minute calculation with your one-sentence reason, the memorylessness comparison with your biological reason, and the simulated mean and variance.
Problem 4 · overdispersion, Poisson vs NB · loads real data

Are the sockeye counts Poisson? Diagnose overdispersion

Load the adult sockeye counts and look at them before fitting any predictors. Parts (a)–(d) are about the marginal count distribution: every day’s count pooled together, with nothing in the model to explain why one day differs from another. Part (e) asks what changes once a predictor enters the picture.

d <- read.csv(here::here("Day2_GLM", "data", "sockeye_adult.csv"), stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
d <- subset(d, Sockeyetype == "Sockeye_Adults")
d <- d[!is.na(d$Count), ]
mean(d$Count); var(d$Count)
  1. Compute the mean and variance of Count. What is the dispersion ratio (variance / mean)? Does a Poisson, which forces variance = mean, look plausible?
  2. Solve for the negative-binomial size parameter $k$ that reproduces the empirical variance under $\text{Var} = \mu + \mu^2/k$. (Use the empirical mean for $\mu$.)
  3. Simulate 5000 draws from both rpois(mu) and rnbinom(mu, size = k) with your $\mu$ and $k$. Compare $P(\text{count} = 0)$ and the probability of a large "bumper run" (e.g. count > 400) between the two.
  4. Name a biological mechanism that would generate this overdispersion in daily salmon counts, and say in one sentence what would go wrong if you reported Poisson-based confidence intervals for the mean daily count.
  5. Overdispersed counts do not always mean a Poisson regression is wrong. A salmon run builds, peaks, and fades over the season, so different days have very different expected counts. Simulate a season in which every day’s count is exactly Poisson given that day’s expected count, with the expected count following a run-timing curve that peaks at about 400 fish: 
    set.seed(2026)
day <- 1:90
mu  <- exp(6 - ((day - 45) / 20)^2)   # expected count on each day
y   <- rpois(90, mu)                  # each day's count is exactly Poisson
var(y) / mean(y)                      # dispersion ratio of the pooled counts

m <- glm(y ~ day + I(day^2), family = poisson)
sum(residuals(m, type = "pearson")^2) / df.residual(m)   # dispersion after accounting for day
    Report both dispersion numbers. The first compares the variance of the pooled counts with their mean, exactly as in (a). The second makes the same comparison after the model has accounted for day, using how far each count falls from its own day’s fitted mean; a value near 1 means the counts scatter around their fitted means just as much as a Poisson says they should, and no more. Why do the pooled counts look heavily overdispersed when every single count is Poisson? (Hint: where does the variation in y come from when different days have different expected counts?) What does this mean for the conclusion you drew about the sockeye counts in (a)?

Problem 5 · interactions I, with and without a product term

Simulating with and without an interaction

Problem 5 builds one idea: an interaction means the effect of one predictor depends on the value of another. Everything you need is generated in the code blocks, so no data file is required.

The idea, stated once. For a two-predictor model, ask what happens to the fitted response when you nudge $x_1$ up by one unit. If the answer is the same number no matter where $x_2$ sits, the predictors act independently and the model is additive. If the answer changes with $x_2$, they interact. That question, find the slope in $x_1$ and look for $x_2$ in the result, is the whole of what follows. You can get that slope by differentiating, or by factoring $x_1$ out of the fitted mean and reading off whatever multiplies it; part (c) shows both.

Simulate two datasets with the same design and sample size. The first is built with no interaction and the second with a genuine product term. Then fit a model that allows an interaction to each one.

set.seed(123)
d_none <- data.frame(x1 = runif(200, -2, 2), x2 = runif(200, -2, 2))
d_none$y <- 1 + 1.5*d_none$x1 - 1*d_none$x2 + rnorm(200, 0, 1)   # no x1*x2 term
m_none <- lm(y ~ x1 * x2, data = d_none)    # x1 * x2 expands to x1 + x2 + x1:x2
summary(m_none)

set.seed(456)
d_int <- data.frame(x1 = runif(200, -2, 2), x2 = runif(200, -2, 2))
d_int$y <- 2 + 1.5*d_int$x1 - 1*d_int$x2 + 2*d_int$x1*d_int$x2 + rnorm(200, 0, 1)   # a genuine x1*x2 term
m_int <- lm(y ~ x1 * x2, data = d_int)
summary(m_int)
  1. Report the four estimated coefficients for each model against the values used to build its data, which are 1, 1.5, −1 and no product term for m_none, and 2, 1.5, −1 and 2 for m_int. In each model, is the fitted x1:x2 coefficient distinguishable from zero?
  2. Write the full statistical model in mathematical notation, giving the distribution of $y_i$, its mean as a function of $x_{1i}$ and $x_{2i}$, and what each parameter is. Explain why this one model statement describes both fits.
  3. Find the slope of the fitted mean in $x_1$. That slope is the change in $\hat y$ produced by a one-unit step in $x_1$, which is exactly what anyone means by “the effect of $x_1$.” You can differentiate the fitted mean with respect to $x_1$. Or, with no calculus, gather the two terms that contain $x_1$ and factor it out, so the fitted mean reads $\hat y = (\beta_0 + \beta_2 x_2) + (\beta_1 + \beta_3 x_2)\,x_1$; whatever multiplies $x_1$ is the slope. Write the slope as a formula in $x_2$, then evaluate it with each model’s fitted coefficients, for m_none at $x_2 = -1$ and $x_2 = +1$, and for m_int at $x_2 = -1$, $0$ and $+1$. Why are the two m_none slopes not exactly equal, given that its data were built with no interaction? And what happens to the m_int slope between $x_2 = -1$ and $x_2 = +1$?
  4. For m_int, solve for the value of $x_2$ at which the effect of $x_1$ vanishes entirely. Compare your fitted answer to the value implied by the parameters used to build the data.
  5. Your collaborator writes about m_int: “the effect of $x_1$ on $y$ is 1.52.” Using your answer to (c), explain in two sentences why that sentence cannot be written for this model, and what it would have to say instead.
  6. Picture each fitted mean as a surface over the $(x_1, x_2)$ plane. First suppose the interaction coefficient were exactly zero, as it is in the equation that generated d_none, so that $\hat y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x_1 + \hat\beta_2 x_2$. What shape is that surface, in one word, and what do its contour lines of equal $\hat y$ look like? Then describe the surface for m_int, and say what happened to its contour lines.
Your answer goes in the R Markdown hand-in. Report both coefficient tables, the model in mathematical notation, the slope formula with its five evaluations, the $x_2$ where the effect vanishes, the two-sentence correction, and both surface descriptions.

Problem 9 · optional extra credit · logit threshold + marginal effects

Occupancy thresholds and choosing a management lever

Presence/absence of hermit thrush at 300 sites is modeled as glm(present ~ basal_area + shrub_cover, family = binomial), giving intercept $-3.0$, basal-area slope $+0.08$ per m²/ha, and shrub slope $+0.04$ per percent shrub.

Two tools you will need for parts (c)–(d).

Marginal effect. On the probability scale the logistic curve is not a straight line, so the change in probability per one-unit change in $x$ depends on where you sit on the curve. Differentiating $p = \operatorname{plogis}(\eta)$ with respect to a predictor $x$ gives the marginal effect

$\dfrac{dp}{dx} = \beta\,p\,(1-p),$

the slope of the fitted curve, in probability units per unit of $x$, at a site whose current occupancy probability is $p$. It is largest at $p = 0.5$ (where $p(1-p) = 0.25$) and shrinks toward zero as $p$ approaches the 0 or 1 ceilings. The intuition is that adding a unit of habitat moves a coin-flip site (near $p=0.5$) a lot, but barely nudges a site that is already almost certainly occupied or almost certainly empty.

The divide-by-4 rule. Because $p(1-p)$ can be at most $0.25$, the largest probability change any predictor can produce per unit $x$ is $\beta \cdot 0.25 = \beta/4$. So $\beta/4$ is a quick upper bound on (and, at $p = 0.5$, the exact value of) the steepest per-unit effect on probability, the back-of-envelope companion to an exact plogis calculation.

  1. For a site with shrub cover = 25%, at what basal area does predicted occupancy probability equal 0.5? Work on the link scale. A probability of 0.5 means the odds are $0.5/(1-0.5) = 1$, and $\log(1) = 0$, so $p = 0.5$ exactly when the log-odds equal 0. The model says the log-odds equal $-3.0 + 0.08 \times \text{basal area} + 0.04 \times \text{shrub cover}$, so set that expression equal to 0 and solve for basal area.
  2. For a site with shrub cover = 50%, what is the new "threshold" basal area? Interpret the shift.
  3. Compute the marginal effect of basal area on probability at the $p = 0.5$ inflection, using $\beta\,p(1-p)$. Confirm it against the divide-by-4 rule ($\beta/4$).
  4. At a low-suitability site currently at $p = 0.3$, a manager can either raise basal area by 5 m²/ha or raise shrub cover by 10 percentage points. Using the marginal-effect approximation $\beta\,p(1-p)$ (or an exact plogis computation), which intervention yields the larger predicted gain in occupancy probability?
Your answer goes in the R Markdown hand-in. Report both threshold basal areas, the marginal effect at $p = 0.5$, the two intervention gains, and one management-relevant sentence.
